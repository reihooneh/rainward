How water moves through a yard

Rain that lands on a yard does one of four things: it soaks in, it runs downhill, it fills a hollow, or it leaves. Rainward's fast model is built from exactly those four rules. Here they are on a single slice of ground.

Why the soil decides so much

A lawn on sand can swallow a downpour; the same lawn on clay soaks up barely a tenth as much, and everything else runs. That is why a rain garden on clay is dug out and refilled with a free-draining mix: it soaks water away about twenty times faster than the clay lawn around it, so a bowl 15 cm deep empties in a few hours instead of days.

Rainward uses typical steady rates for wet ground. Real yards vary a lot: a simple test is to dig a hole 30 cm deep, fill it with water twice, and time how fast the second filling drops.

The fast model: fill and spill

Used to compare hundreds of plans. A priority flood from the edges finds the level each cell's water must rise to before it can escape, which reveals every hollow. Water then passes downhill once, cell by cell: soaking in, splitting among lower neighbours by slope, filling hollows before they overflow. It takes about a millisecond.

The detailed model: the storm, second by second

Used to show you the storm. The local inertial shallow-water equations push water between neighbouring cells by the slope of its surface, held back by the friction of grass or paving. It takes a few thousand small steps; the tests check both models agree on where the water goes.
